Read Your Own Case

What your bankruptcy lawyer filed in your name, and how to check it. A free book, published one chapter at a time.

Published by the Open Bankruptcy Project, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Educational information only; not legal advice. The author is not an attorney.

What this is

Your bankruptcy case is a public court record. It is yours, it costs almost nothing to pull, and almost nobody ever opens it. This book teaches you to read the one that has your name on it.

Every document filed in your case was written by someone else and filed on your behalf. Most of it is correct. Some of it you have never seen. The court has no way to know whether the numbers in it match your life, because the court was never told your life. Only you were there for that part.

Checking is not hard. It is mostly a matter of knowing what the parts are called, where to find them, and what to compare them against. That is what the chapters below do.

Why this book exists

The information already published answers a different question

Search for help with bankruptcy and you get a great deal of it. Almost all of it explains what bankruptcy is, written for someone deciding whether to file. The federal courts publish an excellent overview that says plainly that it is not a guide for filing a case. Trade groups publish guides written for creditors. Legal publishers write for attorneys new to the practice. State bars explain the difference between Chapter 7 and Chapter 13.

You can verify this yourself in about ten seconds, which is the right way to make a claim like it. Search for a bankruptcy guide and read who each result is addressed to.

What none of them cover is the position you are in after the filing is done: the paperwork exists, your name is on it, and you want to know whether it is right. Search for how to read your own case and you will be told, correctly and completely, how to obtain the records. Nothing tells you how to tell whether what you are reading is wrong.

That gap is what this book is for.

What it is not

Reading is not the same as advising

Read this first

This is educational material, not legal advice, and the author is not an attorney. Nothing here tells you what your rights are, what you should do about a problem, or whether anything in your case is legally sufficient. Those are questions for a lawyer licensed in your state.

What this book does is narrower and entirely factual. It teaches you to read a document that already exists with your name on it, and to compare what it says against facts you already know. If the two do not match, the book tells you who to ask. It does not tell you what the answer means.

The distinction matters more than it sounds. A great deal of what goes wrong in a case is not a legal question at all. A misspelled employer, a debt listed twice, a figure that is off by a decimal, another person's information in your file. You do not need a law degree to notice any of that. You need to have looked.

One chapter you will not find elsewhere

What normal looks like

Most people who suspect something is off in their case have no way to test the feeling. They have seen exactly one bankruptcy, and it is theirs. Without a second one to hold it against, an unusual fee looks the same as an ordinary fee, and a case that stalled looks the same as a case that took its normal course.

Chapter 14 answers that from the aggregate public record: typical fee ranges, typical docket length, typical timelines, broken out by chapter and district. It turns the question from a worry into a comparison. It is the reason the data work and this book are one project rather than two.

The Open Bankruptcy Project maintains a searchable record of roughly 4.9 million federal bankruptcy cases, built from public sources and released openly. That figure counts cases in the index. It does not mean every case carries every field, and the chapter says so where it matters.
