Methodology

Data methods: sources, spans, and how counts are computed

The about page promises full methodological detail. This is that page: the four sources behind every figure on the site, the date each one ends, the query that produces a count, the convention behind a percentage, and the questions the data cannot answer.

Published 2026-09-20. Reviewed by Open Bankruptcy Project staff. See the methodology index and the editorial policy.
Home/About/Data methods: sources, spans, and how counts are computed
Short version. Every count on this site is a count of distinct cases in a named source over a stated date span. Counts reproduce to the case. Published percentages depend on a denominator convention that is still under review, so treat the ratios as provisional and the counts as exact. Each source has a hard edge; the edges are listed below.

1. Sources and spans

Four sources feed the site. They are not the same data, they do not cover the same dates, and none of them is a sample: each is a complete administrative record with known gaps.

2. How counts are computed

The FJC file is a snapshot panel, not a list of cases. A case appears once for every quarterly period it stayed open, so a naive row count overstates the number of cases and does so unevenly: long-running cases carry more rows. For Chapter 13 cases filed in fiscal 2008 through 2025 the file holds 18,127,101 rows but 5,097,521 distinct cases, a factor of 3.56.

Every count on this site is therefore a count of distinct case keys (in SQL, COUNT(DISTINCT casekey)), taken from the latest snapshot row for each case. A discharge is a termination code of A or B; that mapping was established by reconciling our counts against six independently published district figures rather than by reading a codebook, and it matched five of the six exactly and the sixth within one case. A prior filer is a case the FJC flags as having a prior filing.

The published population and discharge counts were re-derived from the raw file on August 22, 2026 and reproduced exactly. The headline prior-filer discharge count re-derived to within seven cases of the published figure, a fixed offset that is the same in every window we tested, so the published method differs from the plain query by one small exclusion rather than by drift.

3. The denominator behind every percentage

A rate needs a denominator you can name. Filed cases do not divide out, because a case filed this year has not had time to finish; only terminated cases do. Our published dismissal and discharge percentages are computed as the share of terminated cases with a disposition code in {A, B} versus the dismissal codes. The exact set of termination codes that belongs in the denominator is a convention, and ours is under review: back-solving the published rates lands between the conventions we can construct from the codebook, which means the rule that produced them was never written down. The counts are exact. The ratios are provisional until the convention is published on this page.

One consequence already surfaced. Until August 22, 2026 the district and state pages printed the discharge and dismissal percentages under each other's label. The numbers were right; the labels were swapped. The pages were corrected that day, and the prose that described the denominator as "cases filed" was corrected to say that the count is filings and the rates are of terminated cases.

4. Timelines measure the cases that finished

A median days-to-discharge counts discharged cases only. Dismissed cases, pending cases, and converted cases are not in that median. A district with fast discharges and many dismissals will look faster than it is for a new filer. Every timeline figure on this site should be read as "among the cases that reached this outcome," and where a page does not say so, that is an error we want reported.

5. What this data cannot tell you

We borrow a distinction from our own consistency checks. A contradiction is two present values that cannot both be true. A completeness gap is a value that is merely absent. Neither one says which record is wrong, and neither says anything about anyone's conduct.

  1. Two kinds of missing. In our internal working corpus of about 220,000 dockets, 6 cases show a discharge date earlier than their filing date (a contradiction), while 25,123 terminated cases carry neither a discharge nor a dismissal date (an absence). We report both kinds separately and never fill either one in.
  2. A rate needs a denominator you can name. See section 3. Where a page shows a percentage without naming its denominator, the count next to it is the figure to trust.
  3. A snapshot is not a history. The FJC file is overwritten each release. It cannot say whether a filer ever had a lawyer, only whether the last snapshot shows one. The FJC itself lists the pro se flag as one of three fields it does not quality-check.
  4. A timeline measures the cases that finished. See section 4.
  5. What no public dataset carries. No attorney field (the FJC file has none), no judge (redacted under Judicial Conference policy), no county (district and office only), no plan payment amounts, no fee amounts, and no reason for dismissal beyond the termination code. Pages on this site do not report attorney-level or judge-level outcomes because the public record does not support them.
  6. A collection is not a sample. The RECAP Archive holds the documents someone paid to pull. Any rate computed over RECAP-covered dockets would oversample contested cases. Our case index links to RECAP for that reason and computes nothing from it.
  7. Termination dates from secondary sources. Of 34 cases in our working corpus that appear terminated before they were filed, 32 came from one secondary source, whose date-ordering error rate is about 236 times the primary source's. We do not build timelines on secondary-source termination dates.
  8. Coverage holes we know about. The docket feeds cover three districts since March 2026 with a 34-day hole in spring; the case index ends at filings dated May 31, 2026; the FJC snapshot ends at December 31, 2025. Each page states its own window, and a count that crosses a hole says so.
  9. Names. Search results show masked captions, and caption pages are not indexed by search engines. A person absent from a result may never have filed, may have filed after our last import, or may have asked to be removed. Absence is not evidence.
  10. A holding is not a prediction, and a count is not a conclusion. Nothing on this site forecasts the outcome of any case or characterizes anyone's conduct. The data describes what the record says happened.

6. How to check us

  • Get the same file. The FJC Integrated Database is free at fjc.gov/research/idb, with its research guide and codebook. Count distinct case keys, filter on the chapter and fiscal-year window a page states, and you should land on our count.
  • Reconcile against the Administrative Office. Our calendar-2025 filing count from the FJC file is 575,052. The Administrative Office's published national total for the same year is 574,314. The difference is 738 cases, 0.13 percent, consistent with snapshot timing.
  • Run the same consistency questions. Our checks ask every case the same questions with no filter: is the termination date after the filing date, is a discharged case also marked dismissed, does an event's text use the word its class requires. In the working corpus, 414 cases (0.19 percent) fail at least one, and every failure is reported as a pair of operands, never as a verdict on which record is wrong.
  • Tell us. Send a sourced correction to info@openbankruptcyproject.org or through the contact form. Directory entries have their own accuracy and corrections page. Our editorial policy says how corrections are handled.
